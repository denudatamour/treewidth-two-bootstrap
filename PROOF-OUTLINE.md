# Proof outline

A missing pair is eligible when it is the image of the deleted edge in an injective copy of $H-e$. Extra host edges are allowed. All copies in a round use the same old graph. These conventions matter in Part III, where an unintended copy could otherwise shorten the running time. [Research overview](README.md)

## I. From theta graphs to a whole class

A theta graph has two poles of degree three. Every other vertex is internal to a path and has degree two. Since each path has length at least two, every edge meets such a vertex. The upper bound of Part II therefore applies to every choice of the three lengths. Fabian, Morris and Szabó give the matching linear lower bound for connected rules with minimum degree at least two and maximum degree at least three. The earlier theta proof also determines the closure of $H$ on its own vertices by following the three paths and distinguishing their parities. [Theta preprint](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24227363) · [Lower bound, Theorem 1.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.18786v2#S1.SS4)

## II. How degree-two vertices control the clock

Suppose a full copy of $H$ has a degree-two vertex $v$ with neighbours $a,b$. If a vertex $z$ outside the copy already sees the image of $a$, put $z$ in the role of $v$ and keep all other roles fixed. Every required edge except possibly the one from $z$ to the image of $b$ is present. That edge is present after one more round. This moves adjacency two steps along a path and works for many outside vertices in parallel.

**Count the rounds.** Write $h=|V(H)|$. When a new edge appears with an endpoint of degree below a fixed threshold depending only on $h$, charge the edge to that endpoint. Each vertex can pay only a bounded number of times, because its previously charged edges remain present. If both degrees are large, take a full copy just completed by the new edge and choose disjoint sets of $h$ outside neighbours, one at each end. When some maximal path through degree-two vertices has length at least three, repeated use of the degree-two move completes all edges between these sets in a bounded number of rounds.

In a bipartite rule without bridges, this large complete bipartite graph can absorb vertices along its connected component, taking at most linearly many further rounds. If the spanning component contains an edge within one shore, the two shores can then be filled. For a rule with a bridge or a non-bipartite rule, the proof obtains a clique through the new edge and merges the existing clique blocks it meets. Each merger costs a bounded number of rounds and decreases the number of blocks. There can be at most $n-1$ mergers.

When all these paths have length two, $H$ is a once-subdivision of a loopless multigraph. Here a block has a set of common neighbours large enough to place all the subdivision vertices. Replacing a single midpoint transfers an adjacency across the block. A first edge between blocks then fills all pairs between them within two more rounds. The count charges first contacts or merges blocks when a completed copy spans several of them. This also gives $O_H(n)$ and includes the case of an arbitrary fixed number of length-two paths between the poles.

## III. Why an ordinary graph can take quadratic time

1. **Make the slow motion explicit.** Take cyclic index sets of sizes $p,q\ge 3$ and write $E$ for the set of cross edges between their endpoints. The auxiliary rule has twelve vertices. On each side, two internally disjoint tracks run from a starting endpoint to its successor. Internal vertex types distinguish tracks and orientation. A surviving full track fixes the step even if the other track loses an edge. The two cross edges of the rule join the starting endpoints and the ending endpoints. A missing cross edge can therefore be witnessed exactly one simultaneous step forward or backward from an old one. A missing track edge is already present. One round replaces $E$ by

   $$E^+=E\cup(E+(1,1))\cup(E-(1,1)).$$

2. **Enforce the types with uncoloured graphs.** Replace each of the ten types $c=0,\ldots,9$ at each occurrence by two books with core sizes $20+c$ and $50-c$. A book core consists of a base edge and vertices adjacent to both its ends. Give each core edge 103 additional common neighbours. Even after one edge is deleted from the rule, every surviving core edge retains at least 102 of these neighbours. In the host, an old edge joining distinct designated cliques has at most 98 common neighbours, and an edge incident with a private vertex has at most 49. Thus each book core lands inside a single designated clique. The matching between the two cores places them at one host role. Their opposite size inequalities force exactly type $c$ and fill both cliques. The rule has width two because it is built from a subdivided width-two skeleton by attaching books and four-cycles along edges. It contains a triangle, giving equality.

3. **Check both directions of one round.** Write $G(E)$ for the ordinary host obtained by replacing each typed vertex by its pair of cliques and private vertices, and each typed edge by all edges between the corresponding primary cliques. Localization sends every ordinary witness for a new edge to a typed cross-edge witness. Edges internal to the encoding are already present whenever their other requirements hold. Conversely, a typed witness can be lifted with **any prescribed pair** of endpoints in the two primary cliques. Every newly allowed pair is born in that same round. Hence the ordinary update satisfies $U_H(G(E))=G(E^+)$ for every $E$.

4. **Count the steps.** From the single cross edge $(0,0)$, after $t$ rounds the reached pairs are precisely the points at cyclic distance at most $t$ along the orbit of $(1,1)$. Its period is $d=\operatorname{lcm}(p,q)$, so stabilization takes $\lfloor d/2\rfloor$ rounds. Take $p=k,\ q=k+1$. The starting graph then has $69{,}360(2k+1)$ vertices and takes exactly $k(k+1)/2$ rounds. Consecutive orders differ by a constant. Padding with isolated vertices preserves the trajectory because every vertex of $H-e$ has positive degree. This gives a quadratic lower bound for every sufficiently large order. Every nonempty round adds an edge, giving the matching upper bound $\binom n2$.

This example also locates the difficulty with induction. Every 2-connected graph of treewidth two can be reduced to a triangle by successively suppressing degree-two vertices. Read such a reduction backwards from the triangle to this rule. At some step, subdividing an edge or adding a common neighbour to its ends takes a rule with a linear upper bound to one for which that bound fails. A structural reduction by itself therefore cannot prove the proposed bound for all rules. [Original treewidth-two conjecture](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.18786v2#S8.SS3)
